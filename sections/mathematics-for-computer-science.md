# Mathematics for Computer Science

## What to Expect

This section covers the math that shows up again and again throughout the rest of the curriculum — proofs and discrete structures, linear algebra, probability, and number theory. It's deliberately placed where it is: early enough to be ready before Data Structures and Algorithms, AI/ML, and Computer Graphics all lean on it heavily.

Topics covered:

- Discrete mathematics
- Linear algebra
- Probability and statistics
- Calculus
- Number theory

Honest gap: the two resources below (MIT's Mathematics for Computer Science and Linear Algebra courses) cover discrete math, number theory, probability, and linear algebra well, but neither dedicates real time to calculus specifically. If calculus is shaky for you, it's worth supplementing separately — we don't yet have a dedicated free resource listed for it here.

## What to Prepare

**Prerequisites:** none formally, though comfort with high-school-level algebra will make this much smoother.

A few things worth knowing going in:

- **This is proof-based math, not just computation.** Mathematics for Computer Science in particular is built around reading and writing proofs — that's a different skill than solving equations, and it's normal for it to feel unfamiliar at first even if you were fine at math in school.
- **The two courses cover genuinely different material** (discrete math/probability/number theory vs. linear algebra) — there's no need to do them in a specific order relative to each other, and it's fine to interleave them.
- **You don't need to master this section in isolation before moving on.** A lot of learners find the math clicks better once they see it actually being used — e.g. linear algebra makes more sense once you've seen it drive a transformation in Computer Graphics.
